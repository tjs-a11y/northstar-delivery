How Carriers Can Request Broker Transaction Records Under 49 CFR 371.3

A rate confirmation is not the same thing as the broker's complete transaction record. Federal broker-record rules identify information a property broker must keep and give each party to a brokered transaction a right to review that record.

Start with the current regulation.

Read the current text of 49 CFR § 371.3. The regulation applies to broker transaction records; it is not a blanket right to every internal business record or every document held by a carrier.

Read 49 CFR § 371.3

What the current rule says

Under § 371.3(a), a property broker must keep a record of each transaction. The rule identifies information the record must show, including the consignor; the originating motor carrier and its registration number; the bill of lading or freight bill number; the broker's compensation for the brokerage service and the name of the payer; any non-brokerage service performed and its compensation and payer; and freight charges collected by the broker and the date the carrier was paid.

Section 371.3(b) requires the broker to keep the records for three years. Section 371.3(c) states that each party to a brokered transaction has the right to review the record required by the rule. The regulation permits certain consignor and carrier details to be maintained in master lists rather than repeated in each individual record.

What this means for a carrier or shipper

If your company was a party to the brokered load, you can ask to review the transaction record for that load. The record may help you reconcile the broker's compensation, the freight charges collected and the date payment was made to the carrier. It can also help identify which carrier is recorded as having moved the shipment.

The right is tied to the record required by § 371.3. Do not assume it automatically provides every shipper contract, every broker accounting ledger, unrelated load information or unredacted customer details. Read the regulation and the specific contract before drawing conclusions.

How to make a clear written request

  1. Identify the broker by legal name and MC number, if known.
  2. Identify the load with the bill of lading or freight bill number, pickup date, origin and destination, and the carrier's name and USDOT/MC details.
  3. Ask to review the transaction record required by 49 CFR § 371.3(c) for that specific load.
  4. Keep the request, delivery confirmation and the broker's response in the load file.
  5. Compare the record with the rate confirmation, invoice, proof of delivery, accessorial approvals and settlement or remittance statement.

The current text of § 371.3 does not specify a standard request form or a response deadline. Do not assume that a proposed rule, an industry article or a broker's preferred process changes the current regulation. Check the latest eCFR text and Federal Register notices before relying on a deadline or format.

Use the record to reconcile the load

Match the load identifier first. Then compare the rate confirmation and agreed accessorials with the freight charges and payment information shown in the transaction record. If a deduction or short payment appears on the carrier's settlement, identify the exact line item, supporting document, amount and date rather than treating the entire load as one unexplained balance.

Keep separate records for lumper receipts, detention or layover approvals, TONU notices, claims, cargo deductions and factoring fees. Section 371.3 is not a substitute for the carrier's own invoice, proof of delivery or settlement file.

Do not confuse broker records with an owner-operator lease

Section 371.3 concerns the broker's transaction record. An owner-operator leased to an authorized carrier may have separate document-inspection and rated-freight-bill rights under 49 CFR § 376.12(g) and (h). Those lease provisions are not the same as a direct carrier-broker transaction. See our owner-operator rated freight bill guide.
